## 1. Structure: the 18 parvas and the episode count

### Text size
- BORI Critical Edition (Pune, 1919-1966): 18 parvas, 1,995 chapters, 73,791 verses. Gita Press vulgate: 18 parvas, 2,135 chapters, 92,655 verses. The vulgate is longer because it keeps later-added passages, and chapter numbers do not align between the two. [src: vedapath]
- The longest recension has over 100,000 shlokas, about 1.8 million words. The Bhagavad Gita is Bhishma Parva ch. 25-42. [src: Wikipedia Mahabharata]
- Gita Press publishes the Hindi translation as 6 khand covering 18 parvas. Khand 6 runs from Anushasana to Svargarohana. [src: archive.org / boibipani]
- **The practical difference for scripts:** The popular Hindi tradition (Gita Press, TV serials) includes episodes the Critical Edition drops or relegates to appendices. Examples: Krishna's name as the agent of the endless sari, Draupadi refusing Karna at the swayamvara, and Ganesha as scribe. See section 5. Recommendation [ed]: follow the Gita Press narrative, which is what the audience knows. When a famous scene is a later addition, the narrator should say so gently, e.g. "लोक-परम्परा कहती है…" ("folk tradition says…").

**Planning numbers [est]:**
- **Faithful but watchable:** about 100 episodes × 12 min, roughly 20 hours of finished animation. Adi plus the war parvas (6-9) are about 60% of it.
- **Tight cut:** 50-60 episodes. Merge Virata into 3 episodes and the war into 15, and compress parvas 12-18 to 6.
- **Natural seasons:** S1 Adi (≈22) / S2 Sabha+Vana+Virata (≈25) / S3 Udyoga + war (≈35) / S4 aftermath (≈15) + upakhyana specials.
- **Comparison anchors:** B.R. Chopra 1988 ran 94 episodes × 40-46 min, about 65-70 hours. Star Plus 2013 ran 267 × 22 min, about 98 hours. [src: Wikipedia serial pages] A 20-hour animated version is a deliberate compression.

### Upakhyanas that work as standalone "specials"
Nala-Damayanti, Savitri-Satyavan, Shakuntala, Ramopakhyana, Yayati, Rishyashringa, Ashtavakra, Markandeya, Shibi, Vidura Niti, Ganga-Shantanu-Vasus (better kept core), Amba (better kept core). Nala and Savitri are the strongest standalone titles because they are self-contained romances with strong searchability. [ed]

## 4. VFX and hard-shot catalogue

**Conventions in miniature painting that a 2D stylised version can borrow [src where cited]:**
- **Continuous narration:** one painting shows sequential moments of the same characters. In Kangra this is "typical" (Auckland Art Gallery, Krishna with the Pandavas). A Guler work uses a diagonal to split times and spaces. A Bikaner work uses a vertical river as the divider. [src: Auckland, Sotheby's] Use this for split-screen "meanwhile" shots.
- **Battles:** dense masses of armies with "a hail of arrows" between them. Purkhu (Kangra, c. 1810) labelled protagonists with inscriptions on their armour or above their heads. [src: Sotheby's] On-screen name labels in Devanagari above heroes are period-authentic.
- **Chakravyuha:** the Garhwal c. 1820 painting draws the formation as a circle seen from above, with Drona in his chariot at the centre and Abhimanyu's chariot driving in. Figures vary in scale and the battlefield is an open field without floral ornament. [src: Bonhams] So a top-down ring diagram, possibly a spiral, is authentic.
- **Bhishma on the arrow-bed:** Razmnama folios of 1598 (British Museum, attributed to Ram Das; V&A, by Husain Ali) show Bhishma at the centre, pierced, with kneeling attendants. One version places him under a huge ficus tree, with Krishna and the five Pandavas watching in a rocky landscape. [src: British Museum/V&A/Wikimedia search results] This is a static composition and cheap to animate.
- **Razmnama:** the Persian Mahabharata commissioned by Akbar in 1582. Its battle pages (e.g., Met, "Arjuna Battles Raja Tamradhvaja") use stacked registers of horsemen and elephants. Gods and demons appear in red or blue skin with horns. [src: Met/Cleveland/BL search results]
- **General miniature grammar [ed]:** profile faces, flat planes, a high horizon, gold for divine light. Massed armies are drawn as repeated overlapping helmets and spear tips: a few rows of detail and the rest as pattern. That convention makes crowds cheap.

## 5. Representation and sensitivity

### Text vs later additions (scripts should not state these as Vyasa's text)
| Popular scene | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Draupadi says "andhe ka putra andha" (a blind man's son is blind) / laughs at Duryodhana in the Maya Sabha | Not in Vyasa. In the Sanskrit, Bhima, Arjuna, the twins and servants laugh; Draupadi is absent. Duryodhana later claims she laughed. A later playwright's addition, popularised by B.R. Chopra 1988 | Wikipedia Draupadi, Vedham blog |
| Krishna sends the endless sari | The Critical Edition notes the miracle without naming Krishna. One reading (Ganguli-era text): Dharma, unseen, covered her. Edgerton treats the Krishna version as a likely later addition | Wikipedia Draupadi, Republica, Boloji |
| Draupadi rejects Karna as a "suta" | In some Northern recensions only. In the Critical Edition Karna tries the bow and fails by a hair's breadth | Wikipedia Draupadi, Wikipedia Mahabharata, inkl |
| Fish-eye target seen as a reflection in water | Not clearly in the text; the text describes a revolving device mounted high | inkl (single popular source) |
| Gandhari's gaze makes Duryodhana's body iron (except the thighs) | Not in the original; folk addition. In the text Krishna explains that the thigh blow was deliberate because a fair fight could not be won | Republica, Wikipedia Gandhari |
| Shakuni's dice made from his father's bones, Gandhara family imprisoned | Not in the original; later and folk retellings | Amar Chitra Katha blog |
| Abhimanyu learns the chakravyuha in the womb and Subhadra falls asleep | Described as later folklore | Wikipedia Abhimanyu |
| Barbarika / Khatu Shyam | Not in Vyasa; Skanda Purana (Kaumarika Khanda) | hinduismfacts, Barbarika pages |
| Ganesha as scribe | Excluded by the Critical Edition as an interpolation | Wikipedia Mahabharata |
| Halo behind Krishna | Not in the text (a TV/calendar-art convention) | Republica |
| Draupadi washing her hair in Dushasana's blood | Popular motif. Wikipedia says adaptations popularised it; I could not verify the Critical Edition status | Wikipedia Draupadi |
| Krishna's closing commentary on each episode; Karna-Draupadi romance subtext | Star Plus 2013 format and inventions. The show's network called the epic "open to interpretation" | Wikipedia 2013 serial |

**Writing rule [ed]:** The narrator can tell the popular versions but should mark them ("लोक-कथा में…", "कुछ पाठों में…" — "in folk tellings…", "in some versions of the text…"). This pre-empts the "galat dikhaya" ("you showed it wrong") comment wars that both kinds of viewers start: purists and serial fans.

### Deities
- **Krishna:** do not play him for comedy and do not give him human pettiness. Keep the divine moments (Gita, Vishvarupa, chakra) stylised in gold and frontal, breaking the profile convention. Devotional audiences expect reverence. [ed]
- **The AI-generated "Mahabharat: Ek Dharmayudh"** (JioHotstar, 2025) was mocked for anachronisms (a modern bedside table in Hastinapur, an anachronistic blouse design) and for "soulless" stiff faces. [src: WION, IndiaTV, m9] Lesson: period-accurate props and emotionally readable faces matter more than scale.

### Draupadi vastraharan
- **Approach [ed]:** show the grab, then cut to Draupadi's face and the elders' bowed heads. The miracle is a cloth stream rising and piling up with no skin and no body under it. Narrate rather than dramatise the dragging. Keep it out of the thumbnail and out of the first seconds.
- **Monetisation:** YouTube's advertiser-friendly guidelines treat sexual-violence themes cautiously. Keep the treatment non-sexual and use the word "अपमान" (humiliation) rather than any word for stripping in titles. [ed]

### War violence and YouTube monetisation [src: YouTube help 6162278]
- Animated and scripted content counts as "dramatized". Dramatized non-graphic violence can earn full ads.
- "Severe and shocking injuries" and visibly broken bones get **limited ads**.
- Heavy blood or gore and a focus on blood and guts get **no ads**. Graphic violence in the thumbnail or the first 7 seconds hurts monetisation.
- Content made to look family-friendly that contains realistic weapons or violence can be limited, so do not mark the series "made for kids".
- **Risky scenes:** Bhima drinking Dushasana's blood, the thigh breaking, Ashwatthama's night massacre, Abhimanyu's death, Ghatotkacha's fall, Jayadratha's head. Use the miniature convention: arrows in bodies with no blood pools, fall and cut, and red shown as cloth or banners. [ed]

### Caste-sensitive portrayals
- **Ekalavya:** a Nishada boy, Drona demands his thumb. A modern Dalit and Adivasi symbol. Scholarly readings differ (caste exclusion vs protecting Arjuna's position and Drona's vow). Present the text plainly and do not justify Drona. [src: Wikipedia Ekalavya, synapse paper]
- **Karna:** taunted as "sutaputra" by Bhima and others. Do not make the taunts the voice of the narrator. The Critical Edition removes Draupadi's suta rejection (above), which also avoids pinning the caste insult on her. Viewers are polarised between Karna fans and Arjuna/Krishna fans; give a balanced framing. [ed]
- **Rakshasas** (Hidimbi, Ghatotkacha): avoid tribal-coded caricature. [ed]

2. Classical miniature styles (Pahari, Rajasthani, Razmnama) are an instantly "Indian heritage" look that no competitor uses. They are also immune to the AI-anachronism criticism if props are period-faithful.
3. "What the original text says vs the serial" is a proven comment-bait and search angle ("असली महाभारत", the real Mahabharat).

## 7. Hindi diction list for TTS (65 terms)

Spoken Hindi drops the inherent final "a" (schwa) and often medial ones too. TTS errors go both ways: Sanskrit-style full vowels ("Yudhishthira", "Rāma") sound unnatural in Hindi narration, while wrong medial deletion ("Draup-dī") sounds wrong too. Hindi TTS rule systems are only about 89% accurate on schwa deletion, and morpheme boundaries in compound names block deletion.
